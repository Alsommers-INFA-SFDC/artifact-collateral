Master Data Management

The 4 Ways Companies Implement MDM

Every company organizes its data differently. Here's a plain-English guide to the four main approaches — what they do, when to use them, and why it matters.

MDM (Master Data Management) is how businesses keep a single, trustworthy version of key data — like customers, products, or suppliers — across all their systems. The implementation style determines how that golden record is created, where it lives, and how it flows back to other systems. Click any card below to explore each style.

🗂️

Registry Style

The "phone book" approach — link records without moving them
⚠️
Less Common in Modern MDM Programs
Registry style was popular in early MDM deployments (mid-2000s to early 2010s), when integrating systems was expensive and risky. Today, most organizations skip directly to Consolidation or Coexistence because modern integration platforms (iPaaS, APIs, event-driven architectures) have made bi-directional sync much more achievable. Registry still shows up in highly regulated industries or as a stepping stone, but it's rarely the end goal anymore.

Data stays in source systems. MDM creates a cross-reference index.

Plain English Explanation
📖 Think of it like this…

Imagine three filing cabinets for the same customer — one in Sales, one in Finance, one in Shipping. The Registry doesn't move any files. Instead, it creates a master list that says: "Sales drawer 12, Finance drawer 7, and Shipping drawer 3 are all the same person." You now know they're connected, even though the files live in different places.

Real-World Use Cases
🏥

Healthcare Patient Matching

A hospital has patient records in 6 different systems. Registry links them so doctors see a unified view without migrating records.

🛒

Retail Customer Identity

Same customer buys in-store, online, and via app. Registry cross-references all three IDs to show you it's the same person.

🏦

M&A Data Consolidation

Company acquires another. Before merging systems, use Registry to map which customers overlap across both companies.

📦

Supplier Cross-Reference

Procurement team needs to know if the "Acme Corp" in ERP and "ACME Inc." in Accounts Payable are the same supplier.

✅ Works well when…

  • You can't move or change source systems
  • You need a quick win with low disruption
  • Data governance is just starting out
  • Regulatory compliance requires data to stay in-place

⚠️ Limitations

  • No single clean "golden record" is created
  • Source systems still have duplicate/dirty data
  • Can't enforce data quality rules at the source
  • Doesn't help downstream systems get clean data
When to use vs. avoid
✅ Use Registry when You're in a heavily regulated industry, you can't change source systems, or you just need to identify duplicates and link records. Great for phase 1 of an MDM program.
⛔ Avoid Registry when You need clean data pushed to other systems, or you want to enforce data standards at the source. Registry is read-only — it won't fix bad data at the origin.

Plain English Explanation
📖 Think of it like this…

You have three messy desks (your CRM, ERP, and legacy system). A data team copies the best version of each fact from each desk, combines them into one perfect record, and puts it in a clean central filing cabinet. When the CFO needs a report, they pull from the clean cabinet — but the messy desks are still messy. Nobody fixes those desks.

Real-World Use Cases
📊

Executive Reporting

Consolidate customer data from 5 systems into one clean view for the C-suite dashboard — without disrupting daily operations.

🏬

Retail Chain Analytics

A grocery chain with 400 stores pulls product data from every store's POS into one product master for pricing and inventory analytics.

🤝

Post-Merger Analysis

After acquiring a company, pull customer records from both companies into a consolidated hub to find overlap and revenue opportunity — before doing a full system migration.

🏭

Supplier 360 View

Procurement pulls supplier data from ERP, compliance systems, and risk tools to create a single view for spend analysis and vendor scorecards.

✅ Works well when…

  • Reporting and analytics are the primary goal
  • Source systems are politically untouchable
  • You want a golden record without disrupting ops
  • Building toward a more mature MDM program

⚠️ Limitations

  • Source systems never get cleaner
  • Operational teams don't benefit from clean data
  • Data can get stale if source systems aren't synced
  • Can't prevent new bad data from being created
When to use vs. avoid
✅ Use Consolidation when You need a clean data foundation for BI, analytics, or data lake use cases — and the primary consumer of clean data is a reporting layer, not operational systems.
⛔ Avoid Consolidation when Your sales, operations, or customer service teams also need clean data in their day-to-day systems. If dirty data at the source is causing operational problems, consolidation won't fix that.

Plain English Explanation
📖 Think of it like this…

Imagine a team editor (MDM) who works alongside multiple writers (your CRM, ERP, etc.). The writers can still create new drafts. But the editor reviews every draft, cleans it up, and sends the polished version back to everyone. All writers now have the same clean copy — but the editor and the writers coexist. Nobody owns records exclusively.

Real-World Use Cases
🛍️

Omnichannel Retail

Customer creates an account online. MDM enriches it (adds firmographic data, deduplicates), then syncs back to the in-store POS and loyalty app so all channels see the same customer.

🏗️

Product Data Syndication

A manufacturer's ERP creates product records. MDM enriches with marketing copy, nutritional data, images — then publishes to the website, Amazon, and retail partners. Like PIM + MDM combined.

🧑‍💼

B2B Account Management

Sales creates new accounts in Salesforce. MDM deduplicates and enriches (adds D-U-N-S numbers, hierarchy info), then pushes the clean version back to CRM and ERP simultaneously.

🚚

Supplier Onboarding

New supplier submits data via portal. MDM validates, enriches with compliance data, then publishes the approved supplier record back to Procurement, Finance, and Logistics systems.

✅ Works well when…

  • Multiple systems need the same clean data operationally
  • Source systems will continue creating their own records
  • You want clean data everywhere, not just in reports
  • PIM / product data syndication use cases

⚠️ Limitations

  • More complex integration than registry or consolidation
  • Source systems may override MDM changes if not governed well
  • Requires clear rules on who owns which fields
  • Integration middleware needed for bi-directional sync
When to use vs. avoid
✅ Use Coexistence when You can't fully replace source systems but you need clean data propagated everywhere operationally. Very common in large enterprises with deeply embedded legacy systems.
⛔ Avoid Coexistence when You lack strong data governance to manage field-level ownership conflicts. Without clear rules on "who wins" when MDM and a source system disagree, coexistence becomes chaotic.

Centralized Style

One system rules them all — MDM is the only source of truth
How it works
Records are created in MDM only. Source systems read from MDM — they never create their own masters.
👑 MDM Hub
Records created here ONLY
⟶
CRM (reads)
ERP (reads)
Website (reads)
Analytics (reads)
Plain English Explanation
📖 Think of it like this…

There's one master cookbook (MDM). Every chef in the kitchen must use recipes only from that cookbook. No one writes their own recipes. No one makes changes without going through the head chef (MDM admin). When a recipe changes, every chef immediately uses the new version. The cookbook is the law — and that's the point.

Real-World Use Cases
🏢

New Digital-First Companies

A company building systems from scratch uses MDM as the master for all product and customer data from day one. CRM and ERP are configured to consume from MDM, never create their own.

⚖️

Regulated Industries (Finance/Pharma)

A bank or pharmaceutical company needs ironclad control over who can create/update data, with full audit trails. MDM as the sole authority ensures compliance.

🗺️

Location / Asset Management

A utility company centralizes all physical asset and location data in MDM. Every field team app, GIS system, and work order system reads location data from the one master — never creates it locally.

🌐

Reference Data Control

Country codes, currency types, product categories — all reference data lives in MDM. Applications across the enterprise subscribe to the list and use only approved values.

✅ Works well when…

  • Maximum control and consistency is the top priority
  • You can change business processes to create in MDM first
  • Regulatory compliance requires a single, auditable source
  • Reference data or hierarchies need to be locked down

⚠️ Limitations

  • Highest change management burden — processes must change
  • MDM becomes a bottleneck if unavailable
  • Requires strong IT + business stakeholder alignment
  • Difficult to retrofit onto existing entrenched systems
When to use vs. avoid
✅ Use Centralized when You're building greenfield, you have authority to enforce data creation policies, or your industry mandates strict control. This is the "gold standard" MDM style when achievable.
⛔ Avoid Centralized when You have many legacy systems with deeply embedded record creation workflows that the business won't change. Forcing centralized on a complex legacy landscape often fails due to resistance.
🧩

Hybrid Style

Mix and match — different styles for different domains or phases
The Reality of Most MDM Programs

Real-world MDM is rarely one pure style. Most mature programs run multiple styles simultaneously — different approaches for different data domains, different business units, or different phases of an implementation. This isn't a compromise; it's intentional design. The key is knowing why you're mixing and having clear rules for each domain.

Plain English Explanation
📖 Think of it like this…

A hospital system might use Registry to link patient records across 12 acquired clinics (they can't move those records yet), Consolidation to build a clean patient 360 for their analytics team, and Centralized for all reference data like procedure codes and formulary lists. Three styles, one MDM program — each doing what it does best.

Common Hybrid Combinations
Most Common Combo
🔄 Coexistence + 👑 Centralized

Coexistence for transactional domains (Customer, Product) where source systems still create records — Centralized for reference data (country codes, categories, hierarchies) where one master list is enforced everywhere.

Common in: Retail, CPG, Manufacturing
Phase-Based Hybrid
🧲 Consolidation → growing into → 🔄 Coexistence

Start with Consolidation to prove value and build the golden record. Once stakeholders trust the data and integrations are in place, expand to Coexistence by pushing the clean record back to source systems.

Common in: Large enterprise transformations
Domain-Split Hybrid
🔄 Coexistence for products
🧲 Consolidation for customers

Product data needs to sync back to the website, Amazon, and retail partners (Coexistence). Customer data is only needed for analytics and segmentation (Consolidation). Each domain gets the right style.

Common in: CPG brands, distributors with PIM
M&A Transition Hybrid
🗂️ Registry → then → 👑 Centralized

After acquiring a company, use Registry first to identify overlaps without disrupting operations. Once systems are integrated, migrate to Centralized so the combined entity operates from a single source of truth.

Common in: Post-merger integrations
How to Decide What to Mix
🏷️

Ask by Domain

Customer data, product data, supplier data, and reference data often have different needs. Assign a style to each domain independently rather than forcing one style on everything.

📅

Ask by Phase

What style makes sense to start with now — and what's the target state in 2–3 years? Design for the journey, not just the destination. A phased hybrid is often more achievable than jumping to Centralized on day one.

⚖️

Ask by Risk

Which systems are untouchable politically or technically? Start with a lighter style (Registry/Consolidation) there. Where you have more control, move to a stronger style (Coexistence/Centralized).

🎯

Ask by Outcome

If clean data needs to drive operations (CRM workflows, fulfillment, portals) → lean toward Coexistence or Centralized. If it's for insights and reporting only → Consolidation is usually enough.

Common Pitfall
🚨
Hybrid Without Governance = Chaos
The most common hybrid failure is mixing styles without clear documentation of which system owns which field. If your CRM, ERP, and MDM can all update the customer's address with no defined winner, you'll end up with three different addresses and no one knows which is right. A hybrid approach requires a data ownership matrix — each field, each domain, one authoritative owner.
